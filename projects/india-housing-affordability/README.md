# India Housing Affordability Index

A Python toolkit and Streamlit app for computing home-ownership affordability metrics — EMI, price-to-income, mortgage-payment-to-income, loan-to-value, down-payment savings horizon, and interest burden — demonstrated on a synthetic per-city Indian housing baseline.

## Research Question

How affordable is home ownership across major Indian cities? I want to be upfront about what this project can and cannot answer that question with: there is no real city-level housing-price or household-income data bundled here, and no internet access to fetch any in this environment (see Data below). So this project answers a narrower, honest version of the question: **given a set of financing assumptions, how do the standard affordability ratios behave, and how would they evolve under different multi-year growth scenarios?** Every result here is a demonstration of the method on illustrative numbers, not a measurement of real Indian housing markets.

## Why I Built This

Affordability debates in Indian housing coverage tend to cite a single headline number — "Mumbai's price-to-income ratio is the worst in the country" — without showing the financing assumptions hiding underneath it. A price-to-income ratio of 8 means something very different for a buyer with a 90% loan at 7% over 25 years than for a buyer with a 50% down payment at 11% over 10 years. I wanted a small, transparent, tested toolkit that makes every one of those assumptions an explicit, overridable input, plus a way to see how affordability compounds (or improves) over time rather than treating it as a single static snapshot.

## Economic Theory

Housing affordability is shaped by several forces that pull against each other, and no single ratio captures all of them:

- **Urbanization and land-supply constraints**: fixed urban land supply against rising urban population pushes prices up independent of what incomes do, especially in supply-constrained metros.
- **Credit availability and underwriting standards**: looser lending standards or longer permissible tenures can make the *same* nominal price more affordable on a monthly-payment basis, without the underlying price-to-income ratio moving at all.
- **Interest rates**: a 300-basis-point swing in mortgage rates can change the EMI on a 20-year loan by well over 30%, with no change in price or income whatsoever — this is why price-to-income and EMI-to-income can tell very different stories.
- **Income growth**: if household income grows faster than house prices, affordability improves even if prices never fall in nominal terms — this is the mechanism the scenario projector in this toolkit isolates.
- **Speculative demand**: housing is also an investment asset; price appreciation expectations can push prices above what owner-occupier incomes alone would support, decoupling price from local income for a time.

Because of this, the toolkit deliberately reports *several* ratios (price-to-income, EMI-to-income, loan-to-value, interest burden) rather than collapsing affordability into one score — each captures a different dimension (up-front burden vs. recurring cash-flow burden vs. leverage vs. financing cost), and a household can be constrained by any one of them independently of the others.

## Methodology

Full formulas, derivations, and assumptions are in [`docs/METHODOLOGY.md`](docs/METHODOLOGY.md). In brief: a standard fixed-rate amortizing-loan EMI formula, plain ratio metrics built on top of it, and a deterministic multi-year scenario projector that compounds house-price growth, income growth, and a constant mortgage rate forward from a starting point.

## Data

**This project does not use, fetch, or fabricate real housing-market data.** There is no internet access to real-estate or income-survey sources in this environment, and I did not want to invent numbers and present them as real. Instead, `data/synthetic_city_housing_baseline.csv` is a clearly-labelled **SYNTHETIC** dataset, generated by the deterministic, seeded script `examples/generate_synthetic_city_housing.py` (seed=2024 — re-running it reproduces the file byte-for-byte). Its per-city anchors are hand-picked only to echo *widely-reported, qualitative* relative orderings (e.g. Mumbai and Delhi NCR have the highest price-to-income ratios among major Indian cities; Kolkata and Ahmedabad are relatively more affordable) — every specific number is illustrative, not sourced. See [`docs/DATA_SOURCES.md`](docs/DATA_SOURCES.md) for where real data (RBI House Price Index, NHB RESIDEX, PLFS, AIDIS) could be sourced for a future version.

## Results / Demonstration

Running the toolkit against the bundled synthetic baseline produces, for each of the eight cities, a price-to-income ratio, an EMI at a chosen rate/tenure/down-payment, an EMI-to-income ratio, and a three-scenario (conservative/baseline/optimistic) projection of how many years it would take a household saving a fixed share of income to afford a 20% down payment. **These are demonstrations of the methodology running on synthetic inputs, not empirical findings about Indian housing affordability.** The relative pattern across cities (Mumbai and Delhi NCR showing the highest ratios, Kolkata and Ahmedabad the lowest, in this synthetic run) was built into the generator's anchors by construction — see Data above — so it should not be read as a result the toolkit "discovered."

## How It Works

```
src/housing_affordability/
├── metrics.py       # price_to_income_ratio, emi, mortgage_payment_to_income_ratio,
│                    # loan_to_value_ratio, years_to_save_down_payment,
│                    # total_interest_paid, interest_burden_ratio, affordability_summary
├── scenarios.py     # Scenario, STANDARD_SCENARIOS, project_scenario, run_standard_scenarios
└── data_loader.py   # loads the SYNTHETIC per-city baseline CSV
```

Every metric function is pure (plain numbers in, plain numbers out, no hidden state, no network calls). `affordability_summary()` bundles all of them for one city/scenario into a single dataclass. `scenarios.py` compounds house-price growth, income growth, and a constant mortgage rate forward year by year and reports the first year in which a household's accumulated savings meet a (growing) down-payment target.

The Streamlit app (`app/Home.py` + `app/pages/`) is a thin UI over these functions: pick a city or enter custom numbers, adjust financing assumptions with sliders, and see the metrics and scenario charts update live.

## Usage

```python
from housing_affordability import affordability_summary, run_standard_scenarios

summary = affordability_summary(
    house_price=12_000_000,
    annual_household_income=1_800_000,
    down_payment_pct=0.20,
    annual_interest_rate=0.085,
    loan_years=20,
    city="Bengaluru",
)
print(summary.price_to_income, summary.monthly_emi, summary.mortgage_payment_to_income)

scenarios = run_standard_scenarios(
    initial_house_price=12_000_000,
    initial_annual_income=1_800_000,
    horizon_years=15,
)
print(scenarios["optimistic"].years_to_afford_down_payment)
```

Run the interactive app:

```bash
streamlit run app/Home.py
```

Run the tests:

```bash
pytest tests/ -q
```

## Limitations

- **Synthetic data only** — no real Indian city house-price or household-income data is bundled or was accessible to build this; see Data above.
- **No structural housing-supply/demand model** — the toolkit computes ratios on top of caller-supplied or synthetic inputs; it does not model why prices or incomes move.
- **Constant-rate scenario assumptions** — growth rates and the mortgage rate are held fixed within each scenario's horizon; real rate cycles, policy changes, and income shocks are not modeled.
- **No transaction costs, taxes, stamp duty, maintenance, or insurance** are included in any calculation.
- **Textbook fixed-rate amortizing loan only** — no floating rates, part-prepayment, step-up EMIs, or processing fees, all of which are common in real Indian home loans.
- **Single currency unit, no regional cost-of-living deflator** — all figures are nominal INR; the toolkit does not adjust for regional purchasing-power differences beyond whatever is baked into the (synthetic) city-level inputs.
